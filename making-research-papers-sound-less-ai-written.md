# Making Research Papers Sound Less AI-Written

AI-assisted research writing often feels artificial because predictable choices accumulate. The introduction announces that a topic is important. The related-work section lists studies without comparing them. The method section uses polished abstractions instead of operational detail. The results section repeats numbers, and the discussion inflates modest findings into broad claims.

No single word or structure causes the problem. Readers notice the pattern formed by vague motivation, familiar templates, unsupported certainty, uniform rhythm, and conclusions that say more than the evidence allows.

The aim is stronger scholarship, not cosmetic humanization. A credible paper defines its problem precisely, explains what was done, reports what was observed, separates evidence from interpretation, and limits each claim to what the study supports.

This document works in three ways:

- As a readable article about formulaic AI-assisted research prose.
- As a practical drafting and revision guide.
- As standing instructions for an AI assistant working on a research paper.

## Daily AI Instructions

Human readers can skip this section and continue at [Central Principle](#central-principle).

When this document is provided as context, treat it as the user's default guidance for research-paper writing.

### Objective

Draft and revise clear, precise, evidence-led academic prose while preserving the study's meaning, technical detail, and level of certainty.

### Required Behavior

- Preserve claims, numerical results, equations, notation, terminology, references, citation keys, and required section content.
- Never invent a citation, source, dataset, experiment, result, limitation, quotation, or author experience.
- Distinguish reported evidence from interpretation, hypothesis, recommendation, and speculation.
- Keep specialized terminology when it is the precise term. Do not replace it merely to sound less repetitive.
- Calibrate every claim to the evidence and state important conditions or limitations.
- Prefer concrete methodological and experimental detail over claims of novelty or importance.
- Treat the vocabulary watchlist as a diagnostic tool, not a ban list.
- Follow explicit user instructions and venue requirements when they differ from a general style preference.

### Ten Common Patterns to Catch

1. Generic openings about rapid growth, recent advances, or an evolving research landscape.
2. Contrastive reframes such as "The contribution is not X; it is Y."
3. Stacks of short factual sentences that do not form an argument.
4. Self-answered questions, dramatic fragments, and repeated three-part claims.
5. Unsupported novelty, importance, robustness, or state-of-the-art language.
6. Literature summaries that name studies without comparing methods, evidence, or limitations.
7. Result statements that slide from observation into explanation or causation.
8. Vague attribution, missing citations, and invented analytical labels.
9. Repeated colons, em dashes, formulaic headings, and unnecessary formatting.
10. Abstracts, section summaries, and conclusions that repeat rather than compress or synthesize.

### Revision Order

1. Protect factual accuracy, evidence, citations, equations, notation, and required details.
2. Check whether each claim is supported and appropriately qualified.
3. Separate observed results from interpretation and proposed explanations.
4. Improve the paper's argument, section logic, and paragraph progression.
5. Replace repeated templates, inflated wording, and empty transitions.
6. Vary rhythm and punctuation only where a pattern is distracting.
7. Consult the watchlist for clusters, not isolated matches.
8. Read the manuscript as a whole and remove repetition, performance, and overclaiming.

For a complete pass, use [Full Manuscript Review](#full-manuscript-review).

## Central Principle

A familiar word, conventional phrase, or standard section structure can be appropriate. The problem is mechanical repetition and language that performs rigor without supplying evidence.

Strong research writing makes the problem, method, evidence, and contribution easy to identify. It connects each claim to a result, citation, derivation, or stated assumption. It also tells the reader where the evidence stops.

### Human Review and Detector Limits

Word lists cannot determine whether a paper is rigorous, original, useful, or human-written. Automated detectors compare statistical features such as predictability, word frequency, sentence structure, and rhythm. They do not understand the validity of a method, the accuracy of a citation, or whether a conclusion follows from the results.

A detector score is not a quality measure. Human review must check the research question, evidence, analysis, citations, limitations, and contribution. AI can assist with organization and revision, but the authors remain responsible for every claim and reference.

## Word Choice

### High-Priority Research Writing Patterns

The appendix is contextual, not a ban list. Treat inflections, plural forms, tense changes, and hyphenated variants as one family.

Pay particular attention to these recurring groups:

- Unsupported importance: crucial, essential, fundamental, key, paramount, pivotal, significant, and vital.
- Novelty and performance hype: cutting-edge, groundbreaking, innovative, novel, powerful, revolutionary, state-of-the-art, superior, transformative, and unprecedented.
- Unspecified quality claims: comprehensive, effective, efficient, extensive, holistic, robust, scalable, seamless, and rigorous.
- Formulaic context: in recent years, rapidly evolving, growing interest, research landscape, digital age, new era, and next frontier.
- Ornate abstractions: ecosystem, framework, landscape, paradigm, realm, synergy, and tapestry.
- Inflated verbs: delve, elevate, empower, enhance, facilitate, foster, harness, leverage, optimize, revolutionize, streamline, transform, unleash, unlock, and utilize.
- Empty transitions: importantly, interestingly, moreover, notably, it is worth noting, and it should be emphasized.
- Pompous replacements for "is" or "are": serves as, stands as, marks, and represents.

Use a high-priority term only when the paper defines what it means. A "robust" method should name the perturbations, conditions, or tests it withstands. A "significant" result should distinguish statistical significance from practical magnitude. A "state-of-the-art" claim should identify the benchmark, comparison set, metric, evaluation conditions, and supporting citation.

Do not replace one common term with several unusual synonyms. Technical precision matters more than lexical novelty.

## Sentence Structure

### Contrastive Reframes and Negative Parallelism

Avoid repeatedly creating false depth by negating one description and replacing it with a supposedly surprising one.

Common forms:

- "It's not X, it's Y."
- "X isn't just about Y."
- "X is more than just Y."
- "X goes beyond Y."
- "Not because X, but because Y."
- "Not by doing X, but by doing Y."
- "That's not X, that's Y."
- "The question isn't X. The question is Y."

Weak examples:

- "The proposed method is not just faster; it is fundamentally more efficient."
- "This is not an incremental improvement. It is a new paradigm."
- "The model does not merely learn patterns; it understands structure."
- "The question is not whether the method works, but why it works so well."

Better:

Hypothetical example:

> Median task-completion time fell from 14.2 minutes to 10.8 minutes, while the error rate remained close to 3%.

Replace a generic reframe with a direct, testable statement. If contrast is necessary, define both sides and support the distinction with evidence.

Related prompt: [Contrastive Reframes](#contrastive-reframes).

### Dramatic Negation Countdowns

Avoid negating several possibilities before revealing the point.

Weak examples:

- "Not a minor gain. Not an incremental step. A paradigm shift."
- "Not one case. Not two. Twelve independent case studies."
- "Not occasionally, not partially, but consistently."

Better:

> The procedure reduces [measured cost] by [amount] across [number] test cases under the stated evaluation protocol.

### Self-Answered Rhetorical Questions

Do not pose a question merely to answer it in the next word or sentence.

Weak examples:

- "The result? A significant improvement."
- "The implication? Transformative."
- "The main challenge? Data quality."
- "And the solution? Better representations."

Better:

> The measured outcome changed from [baseline value] to [observed value] under [study condition].

Ask a genuine question when a response is needed. Otherwise, state the point.

### Sentence Stacking

Sentence stacking occurs when every sentence presents one isolated fact. The paragraph has no progression, hierarchy, rhythm, or connective logic, so it reads like a list without bullets.

Signs of sentence stacking:

- Every sentence stands alone.
- Most sentences use the same short factual form.
- Ideas do not build on one another.
- Useful transitions are missing.
- The paragraph lacks voice or perspective.
- The writing feels dense despite using short sentences.

Hypothetical example:

Stacked version:

> The study recruited 48 participants. Each participant completed two search tasks. One task used the standard interface. The other used the revised interface. Completion time and error count were recorded.

Connected version:

> Forty-eight participants completed one search task with the standard interface and one with the revised interface; we recorded completion time and error count for both conditions.

Common causes include asking a model to cover every detail from a large source, combining a large source with a strict word limit, converting notes into prose without instructions about argument flow, and placing too many constraints in one prompt.

During editing, combine related statements, make logical relationships explicit, vary sentence length, and preserve the distinction between method, observation, and interpretation. Read the paragraph aloud to test its rhythm.

Related prompt: [Natural Flow and Sentence Stacking](#natural-flow-and-sentence-stacking).

Excessive standalone fragments are a related form of stacking. Avoid sequences such as "Accurate. Efficient. Generalizable." or "One model. Three tasks. No compromise." unless the compressed rhythm serves a clear scholarly purpose.

### Repeated Openings

Avoid starting several consecutive sentences with the same word or phrase.

Weak example:

> The method uses a fixed threshold. The method removes low-confidence samples. The method then estimates the final parameters.

Better:

> The method first removes samples below a fixed confidence threshold, then estimates the final parameters from the remaining data.

Repetition can work when it is deliberate and limited. Repeated use throughout a piece becomes mechanical.

### Tricolon Abuse

One three-part sequence can create rhythm. Several three-part or four-part sequences close together create a visible template.

Weak example:

> The framework is accurate, efficient, and scalable. It improves reliability, robustness, and usability. These properties make it practical, flexible, and impactful.

Better:

> The framework reduces [measured cost] by [amount] while preserving [quality measure] under [evaluation conditions].

### Superficial Participial Analysis

Do not append an `-ing` phrase that claims significance without explaining it.

Weak examples:

- "highlighting the effectiveness of the proposed approach"
- "demonstrating its broad applicability"
- "reflecting the method's inherent robustness"
- "underscoring the importance of these findings"

Better:

> The measured outcome remained within [range] when [specified condition] changed, indicating limited sensitivity to [factor] in this experiment.

Explain the relationship with evidence or remove the phrase.

### False Ranges

Use "from X to Y" only when X and Y form a real spectrum with a meaningful middle.

Weak examples:

- "From raw data to scientific discovery."
- "From local patterns to global understanding."
- "From theoretical foundations to real-world impact."

Better:

> The study examines data preparation, parameter estimation, and evaluation as separate parts of the proposed procedure.

If the items are merely related, list or describe their relationship directly.

### Rigid Concession Formulas

Avoid the repeated structure that mentions a challenge only to dismiss it immediately with an optimistic conclusion.

Weak examples:

- "Despite these limitations, the method shows great promise."
- "Although the dataset is small, the results remain highly encouraging."
- "While several challenges remain, the framework represents a major step forward."

Better:

> The study finds [outcome] in the observed sample, but the small sample and single-setting design limit conclusions about generalization.

Discuss benefits and limitations in proportion to the evidence.

## Openings and Transitions

### Generic Research Intros

Do not begin by announcing that a broad topic is growing, changing, important, or attracting attention. These openings delay the research problem and can usually describe hundreds of papers.

Common families include:

- "In recent years, [topic] has gained significant attention..."
- "With the rapid advancement of [field or technology]..."
- "As the research landscape continues to evolve..."
- "[Topic] plays a crucial role in..."
- "The growing demand for [general goal] has created a pressing need for..."

Move quickly to the specific unresolved problem, evidence gap, contradiction, limitation, or research question.

Generic:

> In recent years, predictive modeling has gained significant attention because of its broad applications.

Specific hypothetical example:

> Existing scheduling methods assume that every task is known before execution. This study asks whether relaxing that assumption reduces waiting time when requests arrive unpredictably.

The specific opening identifies the assumption and the unresolved research question. A real introduction must replace the hypothetical problem with verified details from the paper.

Write the body before finalizing the introduction so the opening reflects the paper's actual argument.

Related prompt: [Introduction Revision](#introduction-revision).

### Formulaic Transitions and Manufactured Emphasis

Avoid phrases that announce importance, novelty, suspense, or explanation without doing the analytical work:

- "It is important to note..."
- "Interestingly..."
- "The key insight is..."
- "Here is the crucial point..."
- "This is where the method becomes powerful..."
- "The result?"
- "Let us delve into..."
- "The remainder of this paper is organized as follows..."

Some venues expect conventional signposting. Keep it when it genuinely helps navigation, but do not use the same formula in every section.

Use transitions to state real relationships such as cause, contrast, qualification, sequence, or consequence. Let evidence create emphasis.

## Tone, Evidence, and Claims

### Reviewer Responses and Scholarly Correspondence

Generic gratitude and praise can make a response sound mass-produced. In a reviewer response, acknowledge the concern briefly, state what changed, and point to the relevant section, analysis, or evidence.

Weak example:

> We thank the reviewer for this insightful and valuable comment, which has greatly improved our paper.

Better:

Hypothetical example:

> We added a sensitivity analysis in Section 4.3 and now report how the estimated effect changes across three threshold choices.

Use the same principle in abstracts, cover letters, and research correspondence. Replace ceremonial language with information relevant to the reader.

### Patronizing Analogies

Do not introduce every explanation with "Think of it as" or "It is like". An analogy should clarify the mechanism for the intended reader without replacing the actual explanation.

Weak examples:

- "Think of the model as a brain that learns to see."
- "The framework acts like a Swiss Army knife for data analysis."

Better:

> The routing module assigns each input to one of [number] processing paths according to [stated criterion].

### Futurist Invitations

Avoid opening an argument with "Imagine a world where" followed by a list of benefits. State the research problem, the proposed capability, and the evidence needed to establish it.

Weak example:

> Imagine a future in which intelligent systems solve this problem instantly, accurately, and at scale.

Better:

> This study tests whether [method] reduces [measured cost or error] under [specified conditions].

### Performative Authorial Asides

Avoid polished claims of honesty, surprise, or self-awareness that do not contribute to the analysis.

Weak examples:

- "To be completely honest, we did not expect this result."
- "Admittedly, the method is not perfect."
- "It should be confessed that the dataset is relatively small."

Better:

> The sample contains [number] observations, so the estimated effect has wide uncertainty and requires validation with a larger sample.

State the limitation or unexpected observation directly. Use first-person commentary only when the venue permits it and the perspective adds information.

### Unsupported Certainty

Do not assert that a claim is obvious instead of demonstrating it.

Weak examples:

- "The truth is simple."
- "The evidence clearly proves that the method generalizes."
- "It is obvious that this component is essential."
- "The results speak for themselves."

Better:

> Proposition [number] follows under assumptions [A] and [B]; the argument does not cover [excluded case].

Present the evidence and let the reader evaluate the conclusion.

### Inflated Stakes

Do not make every result sound historically important, transformative, or universal.

Weak examples:

- "This result will transform the field."
- "The framework establishes an entirely new research paradigm."
- "These findings have far-reaching implications for all future work."

Better:

> Within the evaluated setting, the procedure changes [measured outcome] by [amount] relative to [comparison].

Describe the likely scope and impact precisely.

### Compliment Sandwiches

Do not add empty praise before criticism, a limitation, or disagreement.

Weak examples:

- "This important study offers valuable insights, but its evaluation is limited."
- "The method is elegant and compelling; however, the comparison is incomplete."
- "This is an excellent suggestion. That said, we disagree with the premise."

Better:

> The evaluation does not compare the method with [relevant baseline], so the reported gain does not establish superiority over that approach.

In related work, peer review, and responses to reviewers, state the relevant strength or limitation directly when praise adds no useful context.

### Vague Attribution

Do not attribute claims to unnamed groups such as "researchers", "experts", "the literature", or "several studies". Cite the source and represent the amount and consistency of evidence accurately.

Weak examples:

- "Researchers widely agree that the approach is robust."
- "Several studies show that performance is improving rapidly."
- "The literature considers this problem largely solved."

Better:

> In [citation], [authors] report [specific finding] for [population, dataset, or conditions].

### Claim and Citation Integrity

A citation must support the specific claim attached to it. Do not invent references, citation details, quotations, datasets, or findings. Do not infer support from a title or abstract alone when the full source is required to verify the claim.

Weak example:

> Prior work has conclusively established that [broad claim] [citation list].

Better:

> In [population or setting], [citation] reports [specific result] using [method or evidence].

Preserve citation keys and reference metadata during revision. When several citations appear together, make clear whether they provide converging evidence, contrasting results, different methods, or examples of related work.

Related prompt: [Claim and Citation Audit](#claim-and-citation-audit).

### Observation, Interpretation, and Causality

Separate what was measured from what the result might mean. An observed association does not establish causation unless the study design and analysis support a causal claim. Likewise, a component analysis, qualitative example, or comparative gain may support an explanation without proving it is the only explanation.

Weak example:

> The shorter completion time proves that the visualization helped participants understand the task more effectively.

Better:

Hypothetical example:

> Participants using the visualization completed the task 2.4 minutes faster on average. Because assignment was not randomized, the result establishes an association, not a causal effect.

Use qualifiers such as "may", "suggests", and "is consistent with" when they accurately reflect uncertainty. Do not add them mechanically to claims already bounded by precise conditions.

Related prompt: [Results and Discussion Calibration](#results-and-discussion-calibration).

### Invented Concept Labels

Do not create analytical-sounding compound labels and present them as established concepts without defining or supporting them.

Common constructions attach nouns such as these to a domain word:

- paradox
- trap
- creep
- divide
- vacuum
- inversion

Examples include "supervision paradox", "acceleration trap", and "workload creep". Define a useful term explicitly and provide evidence, or describe the issue without inventing a label.

## Paragraph and Paper Structure

### Disguised Listicles

Do not turn a list of contributions or findings into consecutive paragraphs beginning with "The first", "The second", and "The third". Use a real list when the venue permits one, or connect the ideas as prose when their relationships matter.

Weak example:

> The first contribution is a new formulation. The second contribution is an efficient procedure. The third contribution is a comprehensive evaluation.

Better:

> We formulate [problem] under [assumptions], derive a procedure with [relevant property], and evaluate it against [comparisons] on [settings].

### Numbered Phase Labels

Do not force "Phase 1", "Stage 2", or "Step 3" onto analyses that are not genuinely sequential. Organize by research question, component, or evidence type when the material does not require a fixed order. Keep phase labels when they describe an actual experimental or procedural sequence.

Weak example:

> Phase 1 introduces the problem. Phase 2 discusses prior studies. Phase 3 presents the implications.

Better:

> The paper first defines [problem and scope], then compares the proposed method with prior approaches before discussing the observed limitations.

### Fractal Summaries

Do not repeatedly announce what will be covered, cover it, and summarize it at the subsection, section, and paper levels. Summarize when the reader benefits from compression, such as at the end of a complex analysis, not merely because a template expects a recap.

Weak examples:

- "In this section, we will explore the experimental results."
- "As we have seen in this section, the method performs well."
- A discussion that repeats the results table row by row.
- A conclusion that restates the abstract without adding scope, implications, or limitations.

Better:

> The gain is concentrated in [condition], while performance under [other condition] remains close to the baseline.

### One-Point Dilution

Do not stretch one claim across the abstract, introduction, results, discussion, and conclusion by restating it with new adjectives. Repetition across these sections is sometimes necessary, but each occurrence should serve its section's purpose and add evidence, interpretation, scope, or consequence.

Weak example:

> The abstract, introduction, and conclusion each call the method "accurate and robust" without defining either term.

Better:

> The abstract reports the main measured gain, the results provide the full comparison, and the discussion explains where that gain does and does not generalize.

### Content Duplication

Check long manuscripts for repeated background, method descriptions, result summaries, limitations, and conclusions. Remove both verbatim duplication and reworded repetition that adds nothing. Preserve repetition required for a self-contained abstract, caption, or venue-specific section.

Weak example:

> The same paragraph describing participant selection appears in both the method and results sections with only minor wording changes.

Better:

> Define participant selection once in the method section, then refer to that definition when reporting results.

### Signposted Conclusions

Avoid relying on "In conclusion", "To sum up", or "In summary" as a substitute for a substantive ending. A conclusion should answer the research question, bound the contribution, acknowledge the main limitation, and state the most defensible implication.

Weak example:

> In conclusion, this paper presented a novel and effective method with promising results.

Better:

> The proposed procedure changes [measured outcome] under [conditions], but its behavior in [unevaluated setting] remains unknown.

### Dead Metaphors

Do not introduce one metaphor and repeat it throughout the paper. Use an analogy once if it clarifies a difficult concept, then return to literal, technically defined language.

Weak example:

> The method is introduced as a "bridge", and later every component becomes a pillar, foundation, pathway, or building block.

Better:

> Define the relationship between the two representations directly, including the transformation and its assumptions.

### Citation and Analogy Stacking

Avoid rapid lists of studies, famous breakthroughs, or historical eras intended to create authority without synthesis.

Weak examples:

- "Study A introduced X. Study B extended X. Study C improved X. Study D applied X."
- "Previous revolutions in [area A], [area B], and [area C] all followed the same pattern."
- A sequence of citations that reports one sentence per paper without comparing assumptions, methods, evidence, or limitations.

Better:

> Prior methods assume [shared assumption], but they differ in [dimension]. [Citation A] optimizes [objective], whereas [Citation B] addresses [constraint]; neither evaluates [unresolved condition].

Use one well-supported comparison when it materially clarifies the argument.

Related prompt: [Related-Work Synthesis](#related-work-synthesis).

### Give Each Section a Distinct Job

The abstract states the problem, method, principal evidence, and bounded contribution. The introduction establishes the research gap and explains why the question matters. Related work synthesizes existing evidence and positions the study. The method describes what was done in enough detail to evaluate or reproduce it. Results report observations. The discussion interprets them, tests alternative explanations, and states limitations. The conclusion answers the research question without introducing new evidence.

Weak example:

> The introduction reports detailed results, the results section repeats them, and the discussion summarizes them again without interpretation.

Better:

> Introduce the research question first, report the measurements in the results section, and reserve interpretation and limitations for the discussion.

Related prompts: [Abstract Review](#abstract-review) and [Method Clarity](#method-clarity).

## Formatting

### Colon Overuse

Colons are useful for definitions, lists, captions, and closely related clauses. Repeated use creates a predictable, instructional rhythm.

Review the draft when:

- Colons appear in three consecutive paragraphs.
- A paragraph contains a colon and the next contribution-list lead-in also ends with one.
- A colon appears beside a contrastive reframe or another dramatic punctuation pattern.
- Every heading uses a title-and-subtitle construction separated by a colon.

Keep the clearest colon and rework the others with natural transitions or direct sentences. Preserve punctuation required by the venue or reference style.

### Em-Dash Overuse

Avoid using em dashes as the default tool for pauses, asides, and pivots. Prefer commas, parentheses, semicolons, periods, or a rewritten sentence when those choices express the relationship more clearly. Follow the venue's style where it specifies punctuation.

### Bold-First Bullets

Do not start every contribution or summary bullet with a bolded keyword. Use bold text only when it helps readers scan a genuinely complex list and the venue permits it.

### Unicode Decoration

Avoid decorative arrows, smart quotes, emoji, and unusual symbols when ordinary punctuation works. Preserve symbols that carry technical meaning, and format equations, operators, units, and notation according to the manuscript system and venue requirements.

Related prompt: [Formatting Review](#formatting-review).

## Prompt Library

Use these prompts during drafting or revision. Replace bracketed placeholders before use.

### Full Manuscript Review

```text
Review this manuscript for formulaic AI-assisted writing. Preserve all claims, citations, citation keys, equations, notation, numerical results, terminology, and required details. Flag generic motivation, repeated templates, unsupported novelty or importance, vague attribution, sentence stacking, inflated interpretation, repetitive punctuation, and redundant summaries. Rewrite only where the meaning remains unchanged, and identify any problem that requires author judgment.
```

### Abstract Review

```text
Review this abstract for problem, method, evidence, result, and contribution. Remove generic motivation, broad claims, repeated background, and unsupported adjectives. Preserve every verified number and condition. Do not introduce citations or details that are absent from the manuscript.
```

### Introduction Revision

```text
Rewrite the introduction after considering the complete paper. Begin with the specific problem, evidence gap, contradiction, or limitation addressed by the study. Remove generic claims about recent growth or an evolving landscape. Preserve the research question, scope, citations, and stated contributions.
```

### Related-Work Synthesis

```text
Revise this related-work section so it compares prior studies by problem, assumptions, method, evidence, and limitation instead of listing them one by one. Preserve every citation and do not infer a limitation that the cited material does not support. End by stating the precise unresolved gap addressed here.
```

### Method Clarity

```text
Review this method section for operational clarity. Identify undefined terms, hidden assumptions, missing inputs or outputs, ambiguous sequence, and claims that substitute adjectives for procedure. Preserve equations, notation, parameter values, and implementation details. Ask for missing information instead of inventing it.
```

### Results and Discussion Calibration

```text
Separate observed results from interpretation, causal explanation, and speculation. Preserve all reported values and uncertainty. Replace vague claims such as "significant improvement" or "robust performance" with the verified metric, comparison, condition, and magnitude. Keep limitations visible and do not add explanations unsupported by the study.
```

### Claim and Citation Audit

```text
List each factual, comparative, causal, novelty, and generalization claim in this passage. For each one, identify its support: a citation, reported result, derivation, stated assumption, or none. Do not create missing support. Flag claims that need evidence, qualification, or author review.
```

### Natural Flow and Sentence Stacking

```text
Revise this passage so each sentence advances the argument from the previous one. Combine short factual sentences where useful, vary rhythm, and use transitions only when they express a real relationship. Preserve technical meaning and do not add unsupported claims.
```

### Contrastive Reframes

```text
Find structures such as "X is not just Y; it is Z", "X goes beyond Y", and "not X but Y". Rewrite generic instances as direct claims. Keep a contrast only when both sides are specific and supported.
```

### Vocabulary and Phrase Review

```text
Review this passage against the vocabulary watchlist. Flag clusters and repeated habits rather than isolated words. For each flagged term, explain whether it is vague, inflated, or unsupported in context. Keep precise terminology and suggest a replacement only when it improves accuracy.
```

### Formatting Review

```text
Check headings, lists, colons, em dashes, bold text, quotation marks, symbols, equations, and notation for consistency with the target venue. Preserve technically required characters. Flag formatting patterns that are repetitive or decorative.
```
